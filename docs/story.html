Where the method breaks

A structural equation model can fit a safety dataset perfectly and still return a number that means something other than what the slide says it means. These six scenes show exactly where that happens, using simulations where the true answer is known in advance.

All data on this page is synthetic. Every dataset was generated from a known model so that the estimate can be compared with the truth. No plant, contractor or worker data was used. Nothing here is a finding about a real site.

Idle

Recovery is not the problem

The coefficients come back right. The spread around them is what nobody puts on the slide.

Scene 01

Recovery is not the problem

The model

Four predictors, one risk score

Unsafe acts, operational stress, system condition and safety response capability each load on a latent and each points at a latent risk score. The generating coefficients are 0.45, 0.30, -0.25 and -0.20. The estimator does not know them.

The estimates

The point estimates are fine

At n = 100 the largest bias across the four paths is 0.007. At n = 5000 it is 0.0014. No improper solutions and no failed fits at any sample size across 300 replications. Whatever goes wrong later, it is not bias in the coefficients.

The spread

The spread is the story

Drag the slider. At n = 100 the empirical standard deviation on the 0.45 path is 0.152, so a single study lands anywhere from about 0.15 to 0.76. At n = 1000 it is 0.041. A single study is one draw from that band, and the slide only ever shows the draw.

The interval lies

A nominal 95 percent interval holds the truth about 82 times in 100, and more data does not fix it.

Scene 02

The interval lies

The claim

A 95 percent interval should contain the truth 95 times in 100

That is the entire contract. It is the reason a coefficient gets reported with a range instead of alone.

The measurement

It contains the truth about 82 times in 100

Across 300 replications, analytic coverage runs from 0.757 to 0.863 and does not climb toward 0.95 as n grows. The analytic standard error is roughly two thirds of the real spread: the ratio is 0.63 at n = 100 and 0.72 at n = 5000. More data does not fix it, because the error is in what the formula conditions on, not in the sample.

The fix

A nonparametric bootstrap restores it

At n = 500 the bootstrap interval covers between 0.925 and 0.975 across the four paths, against 0.775 to 0.85 for the analytic interval, and it is about 40 percent wider. That extra width is not a loss of precision. It is the precision you actually had.

How large the study has to be

Fit costs 153 units. Naming the dominant driver costs 6,884.

Scene 03

How large the study has to be

Fit

153 units buy you a fit statistic

Eighty percent power for the test of close fit at df = 80 needs 153 units. This is the number most study designs are sized against, and it is the cheapest claim on the list.

Precision

369 for a usable coefficient, 1459 for a sharp one

A standard error of 0.05 on one standardized path needs 369 units. Halving that to 0.025 needs 1459. Precision is quadratic in cost and fit is not, which is why so many models are fit on samples that can only support the fit claim.

Ranking

6884 before you can name the dominant driver

Separating 0.45 from 0.30 at 80 percent power takes 770 units. Separating -0.25 from -0.20 takes 6884. The sentence "unsafe acts matter more than system condition" is a different claim from "both paths are non-zero", and it costs an order of magnitude more data. It is almost always asserted on the smaller sample.

A wrong model passes the fit test

Four wrong models, every one of them inside the conventional cutoffs.

Scene 04

A wrong model passes the fit test

Omitted confounder

Drop one common cause, and 0.20 becomes 0.50

With the confounder in the model the path is estimated at 0.173 against a true 0.20; drop it and the same path reads 0.495, a bias of +0.295. The trimmed model has CFI 1.000 and RMSEA 0.000, so it fits better than the correct one.

Direction and equivalence

Point the arrow backwards and nothing complains

Data generated as injuries driving climate, fitted the other way round, returns -0.570 with p below 0.001, CFI 0.9999 and RMSEA 0.004. Cause to effect and effect to cause give identical output, beta 0.4927, SE 0.0159, chi-square 7.696 on 8 df.

What this means

Fit indices test covariance, not causation

All four clear the conventional cutoffs, and omitting a mediator moves the same coefficient from the direct effect 0.182 to the total effect 0.442. A fit index says the implied covariance matrix is close to the observed one, and several causal structures imply the same matrix.

Rare events break the scoreboard

At a 9.2 per 100,000 base rate the scoreboard metric and the deployed tool part company.

Scene 05

Rare events break the scoreboard

Burden

680 alerts for one real event

At a recordable rate of 9.2 events per 100,000 worker-shifts, a model with 80 percent sensitivity and 95 percent specificity raises 680 alerts per true event. Push specificity to 99.99 percent and it falls to 2.1.

Base rate

Identical discrimination, unusable output

The same generating signal gives AUC 0.685 at a 30 percent base rate and 0.692 at 0.5 percent, so the AUC barely moves. Precision in the top five percent falls from 0.629 to 0.016 and alerts per true event climb from 1.6 to 62.

Rebalancing and sizing

Undersampling costs calibration and buys no discrimination

Trained as collected, AUC is 0.711 and predicted risk matches observed to within two percent; undersample to a 50 percent event rate and AUC is 0.707, unchanged, while predicted risk overshoots by a factor of 88 and the Brier score goes from 0.005 to 0.230. Sizing is the same story: 384 events, or 4,175,499 worker-shifts, buy ten percent relative precision, and 668,080 buy twenty-five percent.

The coefficient is not the intervention

Three different causal worlds return the same four numbers and three different decisions.

Scene 06

The coefficient is not the intervention

Three worlds

Same four numbers, three different worlds

The fitted vector is [0.448, 0.306, -0.254, -0.200] in world one, [0.453, 0.307, -0.260, -0.208] in world two and [0.448, 0.303, -0.246, -0.203] in world three. Round them for a slide and they are the same slide.

Different answers

The number you act on is 0.45 or 0.281

Only in world one is the coefficient on unsafe acts the effect of intervening on unsafe acts. In world two the reported -0.250 on system condition is a direct effect; the total effect is -0.070. In world three an unmeasured common cause sits behind unsafe acts and risk, and intervening returns 0.281 while the regression still prints 0.448.

Scale and instrument

The ranking flips with the scale you standardize on

Crew A scores 2.55 and crew B 3.15 on the same raw inputs at both sites, yet standardizing within site puts crew A ahead at site 1 and crew B ahead at site 2, purely because overtime varies more at one of them. Fit the measurement model by site and one loading drops from 0.769 to 0.301, composite reliability from 0.823 to 0.675, and forcing site A's model onto site B moves 4.6 percent of units across the top-decile flag that dispatches an audit.

Limits

What this does not establish

Data

Every dataset on this page is synthetic, generated from a known model by simulations/dgp.py. That is the design, not a shortcut: the failures shown here are only visible when the true answer is known. Nothing here describes a real site, a real workforce or a real rate.

Scope

These are simulation studies of estimator behaviour. They do not show that any particular published safety model is wrong. They show which claims a model of this shape can and cannot support at a given sample size.

Precision

The recovery study uses 300 replications per cell; the bootstrap study uses 40, with a Monte Carlo standard error on coverage of about 0.034. Read the bootstrap coverage figures as approximately 0.95, not exactly.

Status

No manuscript from this work has been submitted or peer reviewed. The code, the generating models and the result tables are all in the repository so the numbers can be regenerated and disagreed with.

Monday

One thing to do on Monday

Take the last risk model your team presented. Ask for the sample size and the interval method behind the coefficient that drove the decision. If the interval came from the analytic standard error, ask for a bootstrap interval instead, and ask what would have to be true about the causal structure for that coefficient to be the effect of acting on it.

  • 1 Get the n. Compare it against 369 for a usable path and 6884 for a claim that one path beats another.
  • 2 Replace the analytic interval with a bootstrap interval. Expect it to be about 40 percent wider.
  • 3 Write down the one causal structure under which the coefficient answers the question being asked. If you cannot, report it as an association.
  • 4 For any alerting model, ask for alerts per true event at the operating point, not AUC.
